# The Cumberland Line

Legacy of Historical Gleanings

Catharina Van Rensselaer Bonney, 1885

Ariantic, or Harriet, my mother, was the fifth child of Col. Phillip Van Rensselaer and Maria Sanders, born at Cherry Hill on September 3rd, 1775. She was lovely in disposition and retained a freshness and youthfulness of heart even in old age. In her elevated social position, she had every worldly advantage attainable in those "soul-stirring times," was accustomed to the luxuries of wealth, and fully appreciated the amenities of her childhood's home. While she did not ignore the gaiety and pleasures of life, she served God with a pure and unselfish heart and always seemed "to walk worthy of the vocation" to which she was called.

Gifted with a lively temperament, ever joyous and happy, with pleasing manners, and a general favorite with old as well as young persons, it was not surprising that she had many suitors. One wealthy burgher seemed the chosen son-in-law selected by my august grandmother. The good lady was well aware of her daughter's preference for her cousin Solomon and that his love was incalculably more precious than the worthy burgher's glittering gold and guilders; but that could not be countenanced. Her gentle and delicate daughter marry a poor soldier cousin? No, indeed. She well knew that cousin was a noble-minded gentleman, but it mattered not; and with a twinkling expression of triumph and satisfaction in my grandmother's eye, which even her spectacles could not conceal, and an ominous shake of her head, she would say, "I will prevent such an unwise step."

Grandpapa De Heer Philip, however, fully appreciated the soldier's worth; he was proud of his nephew, and all his sympathies were freely enlisted on his much-loved daughter's side. Occasionally, a merry twinkle was perceptible in his benignant eye as he quietly took a survey of the tender surveillance of his stately dame. Even in olden times, the phlegmatic Dutchmen sometimes indulged in a little romance; nor was it surprising that De Heer Philip decided that for once the "course of true love should run smoothly."

So, one cold, frosty afternoon, January 17, 1797, the good vrouw was enjoying her siesta and dozing on the sofa by a nice warm fire. Meanwhile, magnificent black Pompey, with his crisped woolly head and shining negro face, his large mouth grinning from ear to ear displaying a set of beautiful white teeth, with a proud and contented mien, had mounted guard at the door of a certain room. Then Captain Solomon Van Rensselaer, with the full consent and approbation of De Heer Philip, was united in the bonds of matrimony to his loved Harriet by the venerable Domine Van Vranken of Fishkill, in the presence of her father and two of her brothers. After the happy ceremony, the domine and the triumphant bridegroom climbed out the back window, and the good old dame was none the wiser. She did not know the gallant and handsome Solomon was her son-in-law until so informed a fortnight later by her good spouse.

Then, like the sensible woman she was, with perhaps a shade of disappointment crossing her bright and good-humored face that her cherished plans were thus unwittingly thwarted, she made the best of what was inevitable and was fully reconciled to the event. She was one who, in disappointments or indeed in every emergency, turned to God as her helper and could say: "Blessed is he who, when the waves of sorrow or temptation are sweeping over him, is able to find the hand of God, and calmly to rest in His infinite strength."

In later years, when her own excellent husband was numbered with the dead, she made her home with this same daughter, receiving every care and warmest love from both of her dear children. It certainly was rather mysterious and did seem like a preconcerted scheme how the good domine happened to be on hand that special afternoon; but he frequently was a welcome guest at the hospitable mansion, and most domines then as now, were never averse to a substantial meal. That day, the clever, quizzical Cornelia surpassed herself in her particular forte, exquisite cookery, and the grand entertainment gave great satisfaction to the unsuspecting amiable vrouw.

The joyful news, however, found its way to the kitchen, causing a flutter of excitement. The grinning sable dignitaries (with all of whom, the "leester kint," Miss Harriet, was a great favorite), loving a frolic, were entirely unanimous in the opinion that dust had been thrown in Grandma's eyes. They rejoiced that their lynx-eyed old missus had for once been outwitted, while they affirmed that the face of the bonnie bride now wore a bright and cheery look. The humorous jokes of the loquacious darkies, who were all "wooly-headed and as black as the ace of spades," were the broadest farce of that eventful day.

After judicious consultation between the mynheer and his amiable vrouw, a marriage portion was given to their beloved daughter, consisting of a fine tract of land which they called Mount Hope. This beautiful site, then in the country in Bethlehem, two miles from the town, was a thick forest; many of the majestic trees were felled, the ground cleared, and a spacious garden laid out with very wide walks. The cultivation of this farm was a great delight to my father, who had an enthusiastic love for agricultural pursuits, and to this Mount Hope, he was brought prostrate and suffering from the battleground of 1812.

Cherry Hill, the ancient homestead, was left by my grandparents to their son Philip P., who was a most estimable and upright man, but unfortunately became involved, and after the death of that gentleman in 1827, the place was to be sold at public auction. A friend of the family, Peter Boyd, Esq., wrote to my father, who was greatly surprised to learn of the fact. At the solicitation of my mother, who could not endure the thought of its going out of the family, he purchased the old mansion with its grounds. Subsequently, he sold Mount Hope to Ezra Prentice, Esq., its present occupant and proprietor, who revels in its luxuriance of beauty.

After only a brief honeymoon, the groom, Captain Van Rensselaer, in March 1797, went to Philadelphia with the intention of resigning his commission, as he very naturally desired to retire to private life and enjoy the happiness of his own cherished home. The beauty of such a placid life did not, however, seem at that time to be in store for him. At the war office, he met General Wilkinson, and when, with the high principle of honor which he possessed, he informed his former commander that he was there with a view to resign, General Wilkinson and Secretary McHenry laid an embargo on that proceeding. They interposed strenuous opposition on the ground that his valued services were required for a delicate, perhaps dangerous duty, which they were aware he would faithfully perform; to aid and protect the commissioners running the Cumberland line. A blank furlough was given to him, with the power to fill it at his own discretion.

Though far from well, he would not refuse; such a mark of confidence induced him to consent, and the heroic, self-denying soldier returned to bid his bride farewell for a season and relinquished all the true endearments of home, while he promptly responded to his country's call for further arduous duties in her service. The noble-minded wife, forgetting self, bade him "Godspeed"; his arrangements were soon made, and he was en route to the army.

# The Last Will and Testament of Philip Van Rensselaer

December 26, 1799

I, Philip Van Rensselaer of Cherry Hill in the Town of Bethlehem in the County of Albany, Being of sound mind and Memory praised be god for the same and Considering that through the Mercies of my Blessed Redeemer my soul shall be received into the Mansions of Eternal happiness do make and publish this my Last will and Testament in manner following to wit:

Imprimis - I will that all my just Debts and funeral charges be paid by my Executrix and Executors herein after named.

Item - I give and devise unto my beloved wife Maria Van Rensselaer my whole Estate both Real and personal where so Ever the same may be found for her maintenance of her [unclear] the maintenance and education of our children together with full power to Lease for a time or forever any part of my Estate, or sell or dispose of any part or the whole of my real estate as she may think fit after my death.

And it is my will and request that no charge shall be made against my son Robert Sanders Van Rensselaer for moneys advanced him either before he came of age and since to this day it having been a free voluntary aid after the death of my brother.

And each of my children attaining the age of twenty one years it is my will and request that my whole estate shall be divided between my nine children share and share alike of all my estate both real and personal. The one third to have no more than the other and should it so happen that any one or more of our children should die before the youngest of my children attains to the full age of twenty one years and leaves a Lawfull heir or heirs before this division is made of my Estate such heir or heirs shall come in for such a proportion as would have fallen to the share of my said children who are dead which are Elizabeth the wife of John Bradstreet Schuyler, Robert Sanders, Killian, Philip, Maria, Catharine, Harriet and Frances Van Rensselaer and to their heirs.

And it is also my request that after the death of my beloved wife that Cornelia the slave her be at liberty to be free provided she chuses to have her freedom or Live with whom she wishes to be entirely at her own choice.

And Lastly I hereby nominate and appoint my said beloved wife Maria van Rensselaer my Executrix and my sons Robert Sanders & Killian John & my son in Law Abraham Ten Broeck, Esquire Executors of this my Last will and Testament hereby revoking annulling and Cancelling any other will by me heretofore made.

In testimony whereof I Philip van Rensselaer the Testator have to this my will and testament set my hand and seal this fifth day of August in the year of our Lord one thousand seven hundred and ninety seven.

Philip van Rensselaer

Signed, sealed, published and declared by the Testator named Philip van Rensselaer as for and his Last will & Testament in our presence who have subscribed our names as witnesses thereto in the Testator's presence & in presence of each other:

Frederick Berger

Johannes J [illegible]

Abraham Lansing

# Chapter IX.

The conviction of Strang was received by the populace of Albany, with the most unbounded satisfaction, and the eager crowds which still thronged the court-house and the streets at the almost midnight hour of the verdict, expressed their approbation of the award in exclamations which almost combined into an applauding shout.-The tension of the general mind, so long delayed of satisfaction, was then somewhat relaxed, and from the blind craving for revenge which before that moment had fixed its absolute requirements upon two human lives, there succeeded a gratified repose, that prompted a reaction of one half its purpose.

The beauty of Mrs. Whipple-had not been unattended with the usual effect of beauty in distress; and those who at the outset of the case had nerved themselves to glare upon her as a misleading fiend, transferred their commiseration by degrees from her dupe, to her fascinating and resigned despair. The interesting simplicity of her appearance, the widow's weeds which added such respectful grace to her condition, and the coarse contrast of the dull and sensual ruffian in the prisoner's box, all seemed at war with the theory that she had been the main agent in the tragedy; and after the strange occurrences that occurred between Strang and the widow, there was some change in public opinion.

The sympathy for the widow (although she was properly condemned for her adulterous intercourse with Strang,) gradually became general; and immediately upon the conviction of the assassin, it took a bolder form, and evinced itself in outright expressions of her innocence of the murder. Indeed, the reflux was so strong, that it even affected the minds of the prosecuting officers, and, as will be seen in the course of the subsequent proceedings, was not without its effect upon the stern philosophy even of the court itself.

On Monday morning, the 30th of July, the day but one after the conviction of Strang, Mrs. Whipple was summoned from her cell to appear before the bar of the court, to answer the awful charge of having been accessory to the murder of her husband.

The court re-opened in the Assembly chamber as before, and long previous to the hour set for the unfolding of its doors, all the avenues to the capitol were thronged with multitudes anxious for admission. At ten o'clock the doors were opened, and almost instantaneously after, the spacious chamber, with its galleries and lobbies, was filled. Presently Mrs. Whipple was brought into court from an adjoining room. Her appearance, though sufficiently dejected, was not so forlorn and desolate as before; for by her side there walked a friend on whom she leaned, and whose pale and anxious countenance evinced the deep concern which he felt in her condition. That friend was Freeman.

All eyes were turned upon the pair, but those who gazed with a mysterious interest upon the man who thus so suddenly appeared in this singular connection, little dreamt of the strange influence which he had passively exercised in the production of the drama that so absorbed their wonder. He knew it, however, for he had long been familiar with the wretched woman's passion and her hopes; and when the details of her crime were first received, he easily ascribed the motive which had baffled all searchers else. He had at first resolved to hold himself aloof from the subsequent and actual stages of the tragedy; but when the final crisis came, he yielded to a sympathy for her sorrows, and gave her the consolation of his presence. Her terrible devotion had appealed to his better feelings. It had wrung a personal respect which obliterated his horror for her crime, and roused his pity, if it did not quite restore his love. The end was now approaching. Alone, deserted, execrated, and contemned, she, who had already braved so much, was now about to suffer death for him; and brute were he indeed, if he would not seek to aid her in her extremity, or to solace her dying hour.

Mrs. Whipple seemed to be conscious of the favorable change which had taken place in the public opinion in relation to her, and as she moved slowly through the avenue which the officious officers opened for her through the spectators, she made the most of the ceremony by bending her head and sobbing as though her heart would break. She appeared in the same dress she wore on the trial of Strang, which was one of deep mourning, and she was also deeply veiled. She took her seat among the witnesses as on the former occasion. Strang, accompanied by his father, was also in court as a witness, and sat at a little distance from the widow. The convict bent his eyes upon the partner of his crime as she advanced, and though it could not be detected whether the widow recognized his presence, that shrouded form seemed once or twice to shrink, and to recoil as if wounded with his bitter and malignant gaze.

It was not until one o'clock that Mrs. Whipple was arraigned. When summoned by the clerk, Freeman gave her the assistance of his arm, but so great was her apparent agitation, that she made two efforts ere she could rise, and when she did and advanced faintly towards the bar, she wept aloud. When it became necessary to interpose the challenge to the panel, her veil was removed that she might confront the juror, "and then it was," says the reporter of the Argus, "that she displayed a countenance once lovely, but now inflamed with weeping and suffused with tears."

The remainder of the day was spent in the attempt to form a jury.

On Tuesday morning, the court opened at nine o'clock, and Mrs. Whipple appeared dressed as on the day before. During the preliminary proceedings, she assumed much more composure than on the previous day, but while listening to the opening of the district attorney, and of his recital of the facts, she evinced more than usual agitation. She had listened with much more patience and composure to the same horrid detail a few days before on the trial of Strang, but at that time it was not her cue to be so wildly moved. Strang was in court as before, and watched the whole of the proceedings with the most intense and feverish anxiety.

The remarks of the district attorney were brief. They embraced a succinct history of the murder, but they were marked throughout with a temperance of tone, a pity for the prisoner, and a pre-apologetic appeal for the necessities of his duty, which plainly marked the extent to which he had been swayed by the temporary public opinion. Having concluded, he called upon Abraham Van Rensselaer, to relate the history of the murder as he had detailed it in the case of Strang.

Before Mr. Van Rensselaer could begin his testimony, however, Mr. Van Vechten, one of the counsel for the prisoner, rose and objected to such testimony being given, insisting that the prosecution could not adduce any evidence, whatever, affecting the accused, she being charged as an accessory to a murder committed by Jesse Strang, until the conviction of Strang was shown, and contending that such conviction could only be shown by the production of the record. He cited authorities to sustain his position, and added that "the trial and finding of the jury in the case of the principal, being proceedings in a court of justice, must necessarily be matters of record, and being thus the highest and best evidence the nature of the case admits of, must be produced against the accessory, ere the trial of the latter can proceed." This was opposed by the district attorney, and a discussion of some length ensued, in which all the counsel on both sides joined. The court, after hearing all the arguments, decided with the counsel for the defense, that the conviction of the principal must be proved before the accessory can be put upon trial.

The district attorney then produced, and read the record of the trial and conviction of Strang, including all the proceedings down to, and including the finding of the jury. Mr. Williams, on the part of the defense, objected to the record as defective, as it included only the conviction, and not the judgment or sentence, and he contended that the record which the law required in the case, must be a record of attainder as well as of conviction. This point was urged at considerable length, and various authorities were cited in support of it; but it was contended on the other hand, by the counsel for the prosecution, that the record of attainder or sentence was not necessary, the finding of the jury and the record of conviction, as such, being alone required. It being at this time two o'clock P.M., the court took a recess of one hour, but upon re-opening, Judge Duer decided that the record of the trial and conviction previous to the judgment was a proper record as far as it went, and that the trial might be proceeded with, without the judgment of the law upon that conviction.

The examination of witnesses then commenced, but during the interrogation of the first, the demoniac countenance of Strang attracted the marked attention of Mr. Van Vechten, who suggested that the convict should be removed and placed somewhere, other than within the hearing of the examinations. The court thereupon directed his removal, and he was recommitted until he should be sent for, his downcast countenance, as he was led out, betraying the extremity of disappointment and despair.

During the remainder of the day, Mr. Van Rensselaer, Justice O. Cole, Esq., and other witnesses, were under examination, and every incidental point which arose was argued and disputed with the utmost force and subtlety on both sides.

On Wednesday, August 1st, the examination of the witnesses was resumed, and Maria Van Rensselaer, Charles Concklin, Getty Sanford, Francis Hill, Nancy Montgomery, Catharine Beranger, Elenor Becker, Henry Schoonmaker, and Philander Fobes were called in succession. Justice John O. Cole was then called again to prove an examination of the prisoner, taken before him on the 22nd of May, in which she made certain confessions, but the introduction of this deposition or examination was objected to by the counsel for the prisoner, inasmuch as it contained declarations made by her when she had not been advised of what she might say as respected herself; that no declaration or statement made by a party, unless the same were made understandingly, could be given in evidence against that party; and that an attempt to give the examination of a prisoner under oath as evidence against such prisoner, could not be sustained.

Arguments succeeded pro and con., after which Judge Duer decided that the examination was inadmissible under the circumstances and excluded it accordingly.

At the re-opening of the court in the afternoon a little episode took place, which, though not directly connected with the case, is of sufficient interest to detail. During the trial of Strang, Judge Duer issued an order prohibiting the reporters, and all persons else, from publishing any portion of the testimony taken on the trial of Jesse Strang, until the trial of Elsie D. Whipple should be completed. It appears that this order had been violated by the New York Commercial and the New York American, and those papers having found their way to Albany were distributed throughout that city, and found their way into every hand. It was during the recess that Judge Duer learned this fact, and accordingly, on resuming the bench, he stated that Edmund V. Sparhawk and Charles King, who had been, on application, admitted within the bar for the purpose of taking notes of the trials, had, in contempt of the court, authorized publications in newspapers circulating in Albany. The court would therefore direct that Edward V. Sparhawk and Charles King show cause why an attachment should not be issued against them for this contempt, and that in the meantime they be prohibited from taking notes of the present trial. As soon as an order was made, Mr. Sparhawk entered and justified himself, by stating that his notes had been published contrary to his express direction, whereupon the court released him from the attachment, and on the following Saturday, Mr. Stone and Mr. King accomplished the same release.

The court then proceeded to the regular business of the trial.

On Thursday evening the District Attorney announced his intention of calling Strang as a witness against the prisoner, but upon the objection of the counsel for the defense, a long argument ensued, which lasted till the adjournment of the court.

On Friday morning, Judge Duer pronounced his opinion upon the question, and stated that upon an examination of the authorities, the court had come to the conclusion that a person though convicted of a crime, is a competent witness in all cases, until the judgment upon that conviction is pronounced against him. It had been the common opinion that the conviction alone rendered the criminal incompetent, but such an idea was erroneous, as it was the judgment of the court, and not the conviction, that rendered the disability in such cases.

But though a convict was by law a competent witness, it addressed itself to the discretion of the court, whether on a principle of public policy, or furtherance of public justice the person convicted shall be permitted to testify against the accused. From the evidence before the court in this case, it appeared that Strang was the principal, that he meditated the murder for the space of six months, and had had illicit intercourse with the prisoner at the bar. That he was a man of thirty years of age, not deficient in experience, but on the contrary artful and deceptive, and the character in which he appeared before the court, was that of the seducer of the prisoner to get possession of her person and her property. The prisoner at the bar was, on the other hand, a young woman, now about twenty-five years of age, married at the early age of fourteen or fifteen, of a character light, frivolous, vain, imprudent, and guilty to a certain extent; a fit instrument in the hands of a designing man.

Had the case been reversed and the testimony shown her as a woman of experience, of strength of mind and energy of character, who had lived unhappily with her husband and expressed a determination to get rid of him—who had selected as her paramour a youth of inexperience, and by the seductions of her person and her fortune induced him to commit the murder, the court, in the exercise of their discretion, would not have hesitated to have admitted him as a witness, and on a full disclosure of the facts to have recommended him to mercy. His case, however, rested on very different grounds, and the court must now say whether the advancement of justice requires that Strang should be admitted to testify at the hazard of entitling him to a pardon from the punishment which his crime deserves. If he is admitted, he has a ground of claim upon the court for their favorable interposition. But to recommend him to mercy was not what the court felt disposed to do. They would therefore refuse his testimony, and if in this decision they had erred, they would have the consolation of knowing that they erred on the side of mercy.

When the Judge had concluded, a strong expression of approbation at the decision, manifested itself throughout the auditory.

A pause then occurred of some minutes during which the prosecuting officers were debating among themselves as to how they should further proceed. Without knowing it, however, they were overwhelmed and converted by the tremendous tide of the reaction in the prisoner's favor, and seized by the new current now so strongly determined by the language of the Judge, the District Attorney rose and observed, that upon consultation he and his associate had come to the conclusion from the facts already adduced, and the remaining testimony in their power to produce, being of so slight a character as not materially to affect the case, the jury would not be warranted in convicting the prisoner, and inasmuch as under the decision of the court, the testimony of Strang could not be admitted, they would abandon the prosecution.

Judge Duer, then, after intimating to the District Attorney that it would have been more seemly for him to have rested on the facts already adduced, than to abandon the case entirely, addressed a few words to the jury, and without recapitulating the testimony, submitted the case to their consideration. He cautioned them to look only to the evidence, and as to the testimony which had been adduced, showing her criminal conduct in other respects, he advised them to say to her, as the Court were disposed to say, and as the Saviour of the world had said to one like her, "Go and sin no more."

The jury upon receiving this charge found a verdict of "NOT GUILTY," without leaving their seats.

Again an expression of approbation was manifested by the audience; but it was immediately restrained. The dejected widow received the verdict leaning forward with her face resting upon the table. She neither looked up nor seemed moved by the abrupt transition in the aspect of the trial, though she held a handkerchief to her face and wept profusely. When the first flurry of excitement consequent upon the rendition of the verdict was over, all eyes were turned upon her shrouded form. The Court seemed fascinated with the rest, but recovering his self-command, the Judge in feeling terms admonished the spectators on the duties of propriety, and charged that no curious and unfeeling eye should attempt to penetrate the thick veil which concealed the countenance of the unfortunate woman from general observation. The acquitted murderess then rose, and with slow and feeble steps moved out of the chamber, surrounded by officers on all sides; and leaning on the arm of the man whom she had now completely won, through blood, against the face of heaven, and in the world's despite.
