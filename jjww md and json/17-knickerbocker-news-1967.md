## Saturday, February 25, 1967

The Knickerbocker News

Charley Mooney

Hanging Was a Public Affair

Some of those South Mall construction workers may not realize it, but they're working pretty close to the site of Albany’s old gallows, where the men convicted of murder were put to death in what closely approximated a carnival atmosphere.

The gallows stood in what was known as Hamilton Hollow, in a natural amphitheater roughly north of Elm Street, west of Eagle Street, and south of Hudson Avenue.

In 1818 the state sent one James Hamilton, who had shot and killed a Major Birdsall, to his death, but only after the condemned man had taken over the proceedings. On the day set for the hanging, an old historical account says, upwards of 40,000 persons from all parts of Albany and Rensselaer Counties were on hand with box luncheons. It was a balmy day in June, and Hamilton gave the big crowd a friendly wave of the hand as he mounted the steps to the gibbet.

The noose was applied, and the trap was sprung, but nothing happened, for the rope broke. Hamilton gave the sheriff an assist by trying out a new rope, drawing himself up by his hands to test its strength. Then, obviously confident this one wouldn't break, he adjusted the noose himself and in a few seconds was dead.

* * *

Nine years later, just as big a crowd turned out on August 24, 1827, to see one Jesse Strang executed for the slaying of John Whipple at Kenwood. The historian recorded that event in this manner:

"At 1 p.m. Strang was taken from the city jail escorted by the sheriff, two jailors, and the Albany Artillery Company, which played a dirge. A wagon, on which reposed Strang's coffin, was drawn behind the group by two black horses. Strang wore, for the final procession, a long white robe trimmed with black; black gloves and a white cap, trimmed with black."

It was obvious the sheriff wasn't too successful with his ropes in that era, for the one that had been tied to the noose broke, just as had the one in the Hamilton hanging. This time there was a longer delay, however, for it hadn't occurred to anyone that this rope would break, and a messenger had to go all the way back to the jail for a replacement. And in the interim, while the spectators broke open their box lunches, Strang took a little breather in a chair someone found for him beside the gallows.

The Frequency of Rope Failures

The frequency with which the ropes were breaking, and the spectacle of a condemned man being forced to sit it out while a new one was en route, led to an official demand that future hangings be moved indoors. It must have been a dark day for the spectators who obviously enjoyed such things, but Strang’s actually became the last public execution.

Another one didn't come along until May 6, 1840, when Jacob Leddings went to his death in the old jail at Howard and Eagle Streets for the murder of his wife, Hannah, in their Bethlehem home.

On January 31, 1851, the state sent Reuben Dunbar to the gallows—again in the old jail—for the slaying of two children in the Town of Westerlo.

The city's last hanging took place on August 20, 1879—this one in the city's new jail on Maiden Lane.

The man who holds the dubious distinction of being the last to die by the noose was Hilaire Latremouille. The state charged that on March 26, 1879, three days after he had completed a sentence in Clinton Prison, Dannemora, he strangled a girl at Dunsbach Ferry. He was arrested at Whallonsburg while attempting to flee to Canada.

Any day now, state buildings will begin to go up over Hamilton Hollow, where the hangman once reigned supreme.
