# Appendix



# Persons of Interest (alphabetical)

Barringer, Catharine: Lived and worked at Hill's Tavern in 1827. Testified at Elsie Whipple's trial about observing Elsie and Jesse Strang staying overnight at the tavern as supposed husband and wife in April 1827.

Bates, Otis: Owned and operated a tavern and farm south of Cherry Hill. Employed Jesse Strang briefly in July-August 1826 before Strang went to work at Cherry Hill. It was at Bates' tavern that Strang first saw Elsie Whipple.

Bates, Otis Jr.: Son of tavern keeper Otis Bates. Shared a room with Jesse Strang when Strang worked for his father. According to Strang's later confession, young Bates told him he had been intimate with Elsie Whipple, though Bates later denied saying this.

Becker, Eleanor: 13-year-old daughter of jailer John Becker. Kept Elsie Whipple company in jail and witnessed Elsie's conversations with Strang through his cell door. Testified about these interactions at Elsie's trial.

Becker, John: Albany County jailer who became the key figure in obtaining Strang's confession. On June 14, 1827, at 10 PM, a distressed Strang sent for Becker saying he was "so full that he must confess or burst." After hearing the confession, Becker made an unsuccessful nighttime search for the rifle. The next morning, with Pepper and Pemberton present, Becker returned to the ravine following Strang's more detailed directions and found the weapon "covered in mud, muck and leaves." As jailer, he later allowed Elsie privileges like an open door during hot weather, which enabled her clandestine conversations with Strang. His testimony about the confession and evidence recovery was crucial at both trials.

Cole, John O.: Police magistrate who conducted early questioning of both Jesse Strang and Elsie Whipple. Took Elsie's voluntary statement on May 26, 1827. Testified at both trials about the interrogations.

Conklin, Charles: Albany livery stable owner who rented a horse and green wagon to Jesse Strang on April 14, 1827, which Strang used to take Elsie to Hill's Tavern. Testified at both trials about the rental.

Cushman, John W.: Present during Strang's prison examination. Testified at Strang's trial about Judge Van Rensselaer telling Strang he should confess since it would "all come out."

Duer, Judge William: Presiding judge at both trials. Made key decisions including ruling that Strang could not testify against Elsie at her trial. Delivered detailed analysis of the case dynamics in explaining this ruling.

Esleeck, Welcome: Alderman who joined Recorder McKown in key prison interviews with Strang. As both city official and trial judge, his dual role provided continuity between investigation and prosecution. His testimony about the prison interviews helped establish that proper legal procedures were followed in obtaining Strang's confession.

Fay, Edward: Albany gunsmith whose meticulous attention to detail helped convict Strang. He recalled Strang coming to his shop at 10 PM on May 3, 1827, wearing a surtout coat "very full in the breast." Fay sold him a rifle for $15, giving $3 and $2 bills in change from the $20 Phoenix Bank note. The rifle was distinctive - Fay had bought a dozen gunlocks from Meachum, put his name on them, and this was the only one he'd sold. He testified he could "pick it out of a thousand" due to peculiarities in its stock. It had been sold once before but returned. This precise documentation of the murder weapon's provenance was key evidence at both trials.

Fobes, Philander: Manager of the Columbian Hotel owned by Elsie Whipple. Gave Elsie the $20 Phoenix Bank bill that was used to buy the murder weapon. Testified about the financial transaction at both trials.

Fobes, Philander: Manager of the Columbian Hotel owned by Elsie Whipple. Became caught in the financial trail that helped convict Strang. On May 2, 1827, he furnished Elsie with a $20 Phoenix Bank of Hartford bill. The day after Whipple's funeral, Elsie confided to him that she had lent this bill to Strang who promised to return $16 but repaid only $6. Fobes carefully noted the specific bank because some were "far more creditable than others" and a $20 bill was as uncommon then as a $100 bill would be today.

Foot, Samuel A.: Counsel for the prosecution at both trials alongside District Attorney Livingston. Argued legal points about admitting Strang's confession and calling him as a witness against Elsie.

Foot, Starr: Bartender at the Columbian Hotel who provided critical testimony about the murder weapon's purchase. He received the $20 Phoenix Bank of Hartford bill from his employer Fobes, gave it to Elsie Whipple who transferred it to Strang who used it to buy the rifle from Fay. About two weeks before the murder, he testified, Elsie told him people were lurking around Cherry Hill and wagered "I'll bet you a hat that he [Whipple] will be killed." When Foot asked if she knew anyone who wanted to kill him, she said "No," and he termed it a "foolish remark."

French, Abel: Foreman of the Grand Jury that indicted both Strang and Elsie. Testified that he had cautioned Strang he didn't need to incriminate himself when questioned before the jury.

Groesbeeck, Mrs.: One of two women who attended to Elsie Whipple when she was ill with chicken pox at Cherry Hill in March 1827. Carried messages between Elsie and Strang during this time.

Hill, Amanda: Visitor from Vermont who helped care for Elsie during her chicken pox illness at Cherry Hill. Like Mrs. Groesbeeck, carried letters between Elsie and Strang.

Hill, Frances: Wife of James Hill, tavern keeper. Key witness who testified at both trials about Elsie and Strang spending the night at their tavern posing as husband and wife. Had a dramatic moment at Strang's trial when she broke down crying after his intimidating stare.

Hill, James: Operated the tavern where Elsie and Strang stayed overnight. Testified about their suspicious behavior and flimsy explanations for traveling together.

Jackson, Dinah: Enslaved woman at Cherry Hill who became a crucial witness after gaining her freedom on July 4, 1827. In late March, while preparing dinner, Strang asked if she would poison Whipple for $500. She replied she "wouldn't send her soul to hell for all the world." On the night of the murder, she was in the kitchen with Maria Van Rensselaer, Henrietta Patrick and Elsie, who sat apart "smoking a pipe." After the shooting, Dinah went out calling the usually vigilant watchdog, who had made no alarm - a key detail suggesting the killer was familiar. Though freed just weeks before testifying, she remained working at Cherry Hill, her testimony unaffected by her changed status.

Lacey, Rev. William: Episcopal minister who visited Strang frequently in prison, accompanied him to execution, and conducted his final rites. Claims he had no part in "The Authentic Confession" published under his name.

Lansing, Abraham A.: River boat captain who amassed significant wealth and became Elsie's guardian after her father's death. When 14-year-old Elsie eloped with John Whipple in 1816, Captain Lansing was "livid" - not just over the marriage but because he had given property and money to Elsie's deceased father which would now be controlled by Whipple, whom he viewed as a "fortune hunter." He went to court trying to reclaim these gifts but lost the suit. His fury over losing control of the estate was so great that he died before reconciling with either Elsie or Whipple.

Lansing, Abraham D.: Elsie's father who died when she was young, leaving her a substantial inheritance that became controlled by her husband John Whipple upon their marriage.

Livingston, Edward: District Attorney who prosecuted both cases. Related by marriage to the Lansings and Van Rensselaers. Warned Strang not to expect mercy for testifying against Elsie. His family connections complicated his role in prosecuting Elsie.

Lush, Samuel S.: Present during one of Strang's prison interrogations. Testified about Judge Van Rensselaer telling Strang that proof of his guilt was satisfactory and denial would do no good.

M'Kown, James (McKown): Albany City Recorder who took a distinct approach in questioning Strang. Rather than focusing on the murder itself, McKown zeroed in on Strang's suspicious tale about receiving five $100 bills in Troy from a man whose name he couldn't recall. At Strang's trial, McKown testified he had focused on these financial inconsistencies because they revealed Strang's pattern of deception. Though present at multiple examinations, McKown carefully noted he didn't recall Judge Van Rensselaer making any specific threats to Strang, showing his attention to legal propriety.

Montgomery, Nancy: Mrs. Hill's niece who helped operate their tavern on the Troy-Schenectady turnpike. Provided crucial detailed testimony about Strang and Elsie's stay during the thunderstorm of April 14, 1827. She specifically remembered the vehicle as a farm wagon with a green box and black chassis. Montgomery noted their conflicting explanations - claiming they were heading to New York via Albany from Schenectady due to a sick child, yet also saying their baggage had been sent "another way." She testified that the pair appeared "very young and very fond of each other," but found suspicious the contrast between Elsie's fine clothes and Strang's working-class attire.

Oakley, Thomas J.: Distinguished defense counsel hired by Strang's father. Former State Attorney General and Assembly leader. Argued forcefully against admitting Strang's confession and later served as Chief Justice of New York Superior Court.

Orton, Joseph: The alias used by Jesse Strang after faking his death in western New York. Known as "Doctor" at Cherry Hill because he wore glasses and could read and write. Used this name until his arrest.

Patrick, Henrietta: John Whipple's niece and a working tailoress who became a key prosecution witness. She shared Elsie's bed when John was away, traveling daily into Albany for her trade. Just before Elsie and Strang's tavern escapade, Henrietta discovered an incriminating note under Elsie's pillow reading "I will meet you at the yellow house, to go to Lansingburgh, in the north part of the city, and wait till I come." Rather than confront Elsie, she showed the note to Maria Matilda Van Rensselaer, who burned it. Later testified that Elsie was "more sociable with Strang than with the rest of the family; nobody else was so sociable with him as she."

Pemberton, Thomas L.: The "backup" coroner found at Crosby's theater on the night of May 7, 1827, when the first-choice coroner Frederich couldn't be located. As coroner, he found Strang's behavior during the inquest tellingly suspicious - particularly his "strange and almost indescribable" conduct and "unnatural degree of anxiety" to blame canal workers for the murder. Pemberton noted Strang's peculiar lack of interest in the extracted bullet that fascinated the other jurors. After Strang's confession on June 14, Pemberton accompanied Becker and Pepper to retrieve evidence, finding the rifle buried exactly where Strang described "in sandy mud, stamped down and covered with leaves."

Pepper, Calvin: One of Strang's defense attorneys and State Librarian. Age 31 at the time of trial. Received Strang's detailed confession and published it as a pamphlet which Strang endorsed from the gallows. The confession remains the most comprehensive contemporary account.

Russell, William James: Grand juror who made multiple visits to Strang's cell, often at Strang's own request. His testimony revealed the jury's psychological tactics - when they found Strang reading his Bible, Foreman French praised this and urged him toward truthfulness. Russell reported that juror Moore warned Strang directly: "mark my words, you will confess before you go to the gallows." Russell stressed that before questioning Strang about Mrs. Whipple, French had explicitly cautioned him that he need not incriminate himself. This testimony was crucial in establishing whether Strang's confession was coerced.

Sanford, Gitty: A longtime friend of Elsie Whipple who had known her since childhood. She lived in Albany at Patroon and North Pearl Streets. When Elsie visited her house on April 14, 1827, Sandford questioned where she was going, and Elsie claimed she was traveling to Troy with Mr. Whipple's brother. Sandford later testified at Elsie's trial about this encounter and Elsie's evasive behavior that day.

Schoonmaker, Henry: served as turnkey at the Albany County jail during the imprisonment of both Jesse Strang and Elsie Whipple in 1827. He provided key testimony about the interactions between the prisoners, including how Elsie would lie on the floor to communicate with Strang through the gap under his door. He also testified about finding copies of poetry in Strang's possession.

Sherman, Josiah: Elijah Josiah Sherman visited Jesse Strang in prison on June 19, 1827 and testified at his trial. He found Strang extremely distressed, crying with a Bible in hand. Sherman reported that Strang expressed deep regret about confessing to the murder.

Stafford, Spencer: provided important testimony at both trials regarding the $20 bill from the Phoenix Bank of Hartford that helped link Elsie Whipple to the murder weapon. He had received the bill from Edward Fay and later passed it to Dr. Townsend.

Strang, Daniel: Father of Jesse Strang and a "respectable farmer" in Dutchess County. Had two wives and fourteen children total - seven with his first wife including Jesse, and seven more with his second wife. When Jesse was arrested, Daniel told his son "if you are telling the truth [about innocence] they would see that no money was wanting to defend him." Hired Thomas Oakley of Poughkeepsie as additional counsel. Came for a last visit before the execution but left three days early, unable to bear the final parting.

Strang, Jesse James (1797-1827): Born in Yorktown, Westchester County as eldest of fourteen children to farmer Daniel Strang. Short, stocky man with black bushy hair and swarthy complexion who wore glasses, earning him the nickname "Doctor." Married Deborah Lounsberry at 18 and had four children before deserting them in 1825. Supposedly faked his own death in western New York and took alias of Joseph Orton. lived partly in fantasy world making elaborate but impractical plans. Hired at Cherry Hill in August 1826 began affair with Elsie Whipple and conspired to murder her husband through various means. Initially maintained innocence but confessed after being told (falsely) that Elsie had provided evidence against him.

Tabor, Mr. Defense attorney for Elsie Whipple at her 1827 trial, working alongside Abraham Van Vechten and Williams. Part of the prestigious legal team assembled thanks to her family's wealth and connections to protect her interests.

Townsend, Dr. Charles Albany physician who provided key testimony at both trials regarding the $20 Phoenix Bank of Hartford bill. He received the bill from Spencer Stafford and was able to definitively identify it in court, helping establish the chain of evidence linking Elsie Whipple to the purchase of the murder weapon.

Treat, Dr. Samuel S. Albany physician called to examine evidence in Jesse Strang's trial. Testified about examining the powder substance found at Cherry Hill but was unable to determine its composition, leaving the alleged poison evidence unsubstantiated.

Treat, Richard S. Judge of the Court of Common Pleas who served as an associate judge during both the Strang and Whipple trials in 1827. Part of the judicial panel alongside Judge William Duer, Mayor John Stevenson, Recorder James M'Kown and Alderman Welcome Esleeck that presided over the sensational case in the Assembly Chamber of the State Capitol.

Van Rensselaer, Abraham Lansing: Twenty-one year old son of Philip P. who became acting head of Cherry Hill after his father's mysterious early death in February 1827. Was in Whipple's room discussing business when the fatal shot was fired. Testified he "thought he saw a face he did not recognize" at the window. Though initially relying on John Whipple for counsel in managing the estate, he came to suspect Strang when the hired man "turned white as a sheet or ghost" upon viewing Whipple's body. However, he said nothing then "because Strang was one of the family."

Van Rensselaer, Kilian (1780-1829) remains one of the more enigmatic figures in the Cherry Hill household. Though he lived in the north side of the mansion and was 47 years old during the events of 1827, he played only a minor role in the unfolding drama. His most notable appearance in the narrative was leaving the house with his gun on the night of the murder, an innocent action that briefly complicated the investigation when Strang tried to implicate unknown armed men in the crime.

Van Rensselaer, Maria P. (b. 1808), daughter of Philip P. and Catharine, was 18 years old during the tragic events at Cherry Hill. She emerged as one of the clearest-eyed observers of her cousin Elsie Whipple's character, describing her as "weak" and "easily discouraged." Her presence in the household during the murder and its aftermath provided investigators with valuable insights into the dynamics between Elsie and Jesse Strang. Maria's clear-headed testimony helped establish the pattern of behavior that led to the fatal events.

Van Rensselaer, Maria Sanders (1749-1830), known simply as "the old lady" by 1827, wielded considerable influence from her domain in the north side of Cherry Hill. The widow of Philip Van Rensselaer maintained her right of occupancy after his death, presiding over her own parlor and bedroom furnished with pieces inherited from her father. A mother of thirteen children, nine of whom survived to adulthood, she was known for her strong will and deep religious convictions. Though often in the background of the murder narrative, her presence added another layer of complexity to the household dynamics.

Van Rensselaer, Philip P. (1783-1827) never achieved the business success of his father. Living on the south side of Cherry Hill with his wife Catharine Lansing (his first cousin) and their four children, he struggled financially and was near bankruptcy when he died mysteriously in February 1827, just months before the murder. His death left the household without a strong patriarch and may have contributed to the circumstances that allowed the tragedy to unfold. He was survived by his children Abraham, Maria P., Elsie, and Arriette.

Van Rensselaer, Philip K. (1747-1798) established the family at Cherry Hill, building the mansion in 1787. A cousin of the Patroon, he made his fortune as a merchant, farmer, and tanner, showing particular business acumen in his role as Commissary of Military Stores during the Revolution. He married Maria Sanders, with whom he had thirteen children, nine surviving to adulthood. His shrewd business sense and prosperity stood in stark contrast to his son Philip P.'s later financial struggles. Though he died in 1798, long before the murder, his legacy shaped the household where the tragedy would later unfold.

Van Rensselaer, Arriete (b. 1818) was the youngest child of Philip P. and Catharine Van Rensselaer, only eight years old when her father died and nine during the murder that shook her household. She lived on the south side of Cherry Hill with her mother and siblings during this tumultuous period, though her young age means she appears only peripherally in accounts of the events.

Van Rensselaer, Catharine Lansing was the wife of Philip P. Van Rensselaer and daughter of old Abraham A. Lansing, a successful river captain. She was both wife and first cousin to Philip P., exemplifying the close intermarriage of Albany's leading families. Following her husband's sudden death in February 1827, she remained at Cherry Hill with her children. During the murder investigation and trials, she was referred to as "Aunt Caty" and, though she never testified, appears to have been a strong force of disapproval toward her niece Elsie Whipple. She refused to comfort Elsie during her hysterics after Strang's arrest, having told her she was "a bad woman and might possibly hang."

Van Rensselaer, Elsie (b. 1816) was one of the younger children of Philip P. and Catharine, ten years old at the time of the murder. Like her sister Arriete, she lived on the south side of Cherry Hill. The documents note that she sometimes carried letters between Elsie Whipple and Jesse Strang, though as a child she was likely unaware of the significance of these errands.

Van Rensselaer, Judge John S. played a significant role in the investigation of the murder. He examined Strang on May 11 and pressed him hard for a confession, telling him "he had better make the best use of his time." When Strang protested his innocence, the Judge and others investigating the case used various strategies, including telling Strang that Mrs. Whipple had provided evidence against him, to try to obtain a confession.

Van Rensselaer, Maria Matilda (1787-1856) occupied a unique position in the Cherry Hill household as an unmarried adult sister of Philip P. Van Rensselaer. She lived in the north half of the house with her mother, Maria Sanders Van Rensselaer, maintaining a degree of independence while remaining within the family sphere. She played a crucial role in uncovering the relationship between Elsie Whipple and Jesse Strang when Henrietta Patrick brought her the incriminating note found under Elsie's pillow, which Maria Matilda promptly burned.

Van Rensselaer, Stephen served on the grand jury that indicted Strang and was among those who visited him in jail. He later testified at Strang's trial as part of the committee of six grand jurors who questioned the accused. His testimony helped establish the circumstances under which Strang finally confessed to the murder, though he maintained he had not heard anyone directly advise Strang to confess, contrary to other witnesses' recollections.

Van Vechten, Abraham: Principal defense counsel for Elsie Whipple at her 1827 trial. A leading member of the Albany bar and relative of Elsie's, he successfully argued against allowing Jesse Strang to testify against her. His family connections and legal prowess were instrumental in securing her acquittal despite considerable evidence of her involvement in the murder plot.

Whipple, Abraham (1821-1890): Six-year-old son of John and Elsie Whipple at the time of his father's murder. Was given sulphur mixed with arsenic by his mother during one of her attempts to poison John Whipple but survived. After the murder, his mother placed the Columbian Hotel in trust for his maintenance and education.

Whipple, Melancton: Nephew of murder victim John Whipple who was present in Albany around the time of the murder, having dined with his uncle at Fobes' tavern on May 7, 1827. Over the following days, had several revealing conversations with Elsie Whipple in which she gradually betrayed knowledge of the murder plot through contradictory statements about Strang. Initially defended Strang as "one of John's best friends" but later admitted he had threatened to "have her if it cost him his life." After becoming convinced of her guilt, he ceased visiting her. Testified about these conversations at her trial.

Whipple, John (1792-1827): New England-born merchant and contractor who rose from humble beginnings as a river sloop captain to become a successful businessman. At age 24, married 14-year-old Elsie Lansing against her family's wishes, later winning control of her inheritance through legal action. Known for his industry, prudence and fair dealing in business affairs, particularly in contracts for the Delaware & Hudson Canal. Though patient with his unstable wife and protective of her interests, their marriage was strained by her frivolity and his frequent absences. Lodged at Cherry Hill with his wife, young son Abraham, and niece Henrietta Patrick. Shot through his window on the evening of May 7, 1827 while reviewing accounts with Abraham Van Rensselaer. Was 34 at the time of his murder.

Whipple, Elsie Lansing (1802-1832): Beautiful but emotionally unstable woman born into Albany's elite Lansing family. Raised by indulgent mother and grandmother who pampered her and failed to instill moral principles. At age 14, eloped with John Whipple, nine years her senior, against her grandfather's wishes. Though married young and inheriting considerable property, she proved an erratic, undisciplined wife prone to hysteria and attention-seeking behavior. In 1826-27, began an affair with hired hand Jesse Strang at Cherry Hill, conspiring with him to murder her husband through various means before he finally shot John Whipple. Though clearly implicated, she was acquitted at trial due to her family's influence. Remarried Nathaniel Freeman in New Jersey in 1829 after Strang's execution. Moved to Onondaga County after Freeman's death, where records of her cease. It is believed and died there in 1832 at age 30. Her son Abraham inherited the Columbian Hotel she had placed in trust for him and became a lawyer.

Whipple, Melancton: Nephew of John Whipple who was present in Albany around the time of the murder, having dined with his uncle at Fobes' tavern on May 7, 1827. Over a ten-day period following the murder, had several revealing conversations with Elsie Whipple, during which she gradually revealed more about her relationship with Strang - from initially defending him as "one of John's best friends" to finally admitting he had threatened to "have her if it cost him his life." After becoming convinced of her guilt based on these conversations, he ceased visiting her. Testified about these interactions at her trial.

Williams, John R.: Prison visitor who spoke with Jesse Strang and testified at his trial about asking Strang why he hadn't confessed earlier. Strang told him he had delayed because he hoped Elsie Whipple would bring it out rather than him, but that his feelings eventually became such that he could refrain no longer.

Williams, Mr.: Defense attorney who represented Elsie Whipple alongside Abraham Van Vechten and Tabor at her 1827 trial. During the proceedings, petitioned the court to prevent anyone from having communication with Strang during the trial period except for his counsel, relatives, the jailer and public officers.

Wilson, George: Hired laborer at Cherry Hill who shared sleeping quarters with Jesse Strang in the cellar rooms until April 1827. Brother of William Wilson. Left Cherry Hill shortly before the murder, when Strang then became the only hired man rooming with William Wilson.

Wilson, William: Hired laborer at Cherry Hill and brother of George Wilson. Slept in the cellar rooms near Strang. Notable for a key conversation with Strang where they were on the hill behind Cherry Hill and Strang remarked it would be "a fine place to shoot into the window." The morning after the murder, Wilson asked Strang if he remembered that conversation, causing Strang to get up from the breakfast table and leave. Wilson also kept gunpowder in his room which Elsie Whipple later accessed to give to Strang.

Wing, Dr. Joel A.: Albany physician who served as the Whipple family doctor. Called to Cherry Hill the morning after the murder to assist the coroner and extract the bullet from John Whipple's body, which he found had passed through the left shoulder blade, severed a major artery near the heart, and lodged in the right lung. Testified at both trials about the bullet wound and his interactions with the suspects - notably a conversation where Elsie complained to him that her relatives were turning against her and implying she was involved in her husband's death. Also identified the bullet in court as matching the type that would have been fired from Strang's rifle.

# Timeline of Events

• May 7, 1827: John Whipple is assassinated at his Cherry Hill residence in Albany, New York. Shot through a window, the bullet enters his shoulder, severs an artery, and lodges in his lung. He dies almost instantly.

• May 8, 1827: Mayor James Stevenson offers a $500 reward for the apprehension of Whipple's killer.

• May 9, 1827: A funeral for John Whipple is held at Cherry Hill. Public reaction grows, and investigations are underway with examinations of witnesses.

• May 10, 1827: Governor DeWitt Clinton amplifies the reward for capturing the murderer.

• May 12-14, 1827: Jesse Strang, using the alias Joseph Orton, is arrested and examined. Initial evidence points to him as the primary suspect.

• May 15, 1827: Jesse Strang's true identity is revealed during further investigations.

• May 16, 1827: Reports emerge that Jesse Strang faked his death years earlier, building a history of deceit.

• May 17, 1827: Public speculation grows around the involvement of Elsie Whipple, though no conclusive evidence ties her directly to the crime.

• July 4, 1827: Emancipation Day in New York marks the abolition of slavery for those born before July 1799. This event sets a broader societal context, contrasting the grim events at Cherry Hill.

• July 24, 1827: Jesse Strang's trial begins in Albany. The court hears detailed accounts of his relationship with Elsie and the events leading to the murder.

• July 25, 1827: The district attorney presents the prosecution's case, asserting that Strang's relationship with Elsie motivated him to commit the murder.

• August 4, 1827: Jesse Strang confesses, offering a detailed account of his relationship with Elsie, her involvement, and the murder plot. This confession is later published.

• August 1827: Jesse Strang is sentenced to death. Public sentiment and the massive turnout for his execution highlight the societal impact of this case.
