Mr. Livingston called for Jesse Strang.

By the court. You cannot call Strang upon the stand without an order from the court, Mr. Livingston then moved that Jesse Strang be called into court to testify in this cause.

Mr. Van Vechten, on behalf of the prisoner, objected, inasmuch as Strang had been convicted as principal, of an infamous crime, and was therefore by law rendered incompetent as a witness to testify in any cause.

Mr. Van Vechten also contended that if used as a witness he must become a subject of executive clemency. Mr. Van Vechten having opened the argument upon the objection, and cited two or three authorities, said the counsel for the prisoner would reserve themselves until after the counsel for the prosecution should have been heard in support of the motion.

The District Attorney spoke in reply, and in regard to the first objection, contended that it is not the conviction for an infamous crime, but the sentence, which creates the disqualification. In the present case, the sentence had not yet been pronounced, and Strang is therefore a competent witness.

As it regarded the second point, viz., the claims which being used as a witness would give Strang to a pardon, the District Attorney maintained that it did not necessarily follow; that would be a subject altogether for the consideration of the executive.

He must act upon his own responsibility, and judge whether the circumstances would in any respect render it his duty to extend his clemency.

Mr. Livingston did not hesitate to express his opinion upon the subject, and he had consulted able counsel, and his conviction was, that if Strang should be called upon the stand, and was solemnly premonished that he need not under any circumstances hope for a pardon in this world (as was done in the case of Hodges, in Orange County) he would not be entitled to any mercy whatever.

It being now past 8 o'clock the court directed a recess until to-morrow morning, at 10 o'clock, when the opinion upon this important question will be pronounced.

## Friday, August 3, 1827

THE PEOPLE v. ELISE WHIPPLE

On the opening of the court this morning, Judge Duer pronounced the opinion of the court upon the question raised and argued last night, whether Strang, the principal in the felony, and wherewith the accused stood charged as an accessory, should be admitted to testify against the accused.

The Judges stated, that upon examination of the authorities, the court had come to the conclusion that there was no doubt but that a person, though convicted of a crime, is a competent witness in all cases, until the judgment upon that conviction is pronounced against him.

It has been said that the common opinion was, that a conviction alone rendered him incompetent; but that, the court said, was not the law. It is not the conviction, but the judgment, that creates the disability in such cases.

As an accomplice, Strang is a competent witness. Unless the conviction and judgment are proved, a witness is not incompetent from infamy of character, though he may confess himself guilty of an infamous crime.

This case then addressed itself to the discretion of the court, not to their judgment as to the competency of the witness, but to their discretion whether, on a principle of public policy, and in furtherance of public justice, the person convicted shall be permitted to testify against the accused.

From the evidence before the court, it appeared that Strang meditated the murder he committed for the space of six months; that he had an illicit intercourse with the prisoner at the bar; and that he had expressed himself determined to have her if it cost him his life.

He is a man of about 30 years of age, not deficient in experience, on the contrary artful and deceptive, passing himself off as an unmarried man, and under a false and assumed name.

The character in which he appears before the court as to his participation in the crime which has been committed, is not as an instrument used by the prisoner to get rid of her husband, but the seducer of the prisoner to obtain possession of her person and property.

The prisoner at the bar appears as a young woman, now about 25 years of age, married at the early age of 14 to her late husband, possessed of property to a considerable amount, of a character light, frivolous, weak, vain, imprudent and wicked, and guilty to a certain degree; a fit instrument in the hands of a designing man, but destitute of those qualities which might be supposed to have swayed the mind or controlled the actions of the person with whom she had had an illicit intercourse.

Had the case been reversed, and she presented as a woman of experience, of strength of mind and energy of character, who had lived unhappily with her husband, and expressed a determination to get rid of him—who had selected as her paramour a youth of inexperience, and by the seductions of her person and her fortune had induced him to commit the murder, in the exercise of their discretion the court would not have hesitated to admit him as a witness, and in a full disclosure of the facts to have recommended him to mercy.

The Judge therefore decided that Jesse Strang cannot be admitted as a witness.

After a few moments, the district attorney arose and observed, that as the testimony already produced would not warrant the jury in convicting the prisoner, and inasmuch as under the decision of the court the testimony of Strang could not be admitted, he would abandon the prosecution.

Judge Duer.

That is a matter in the discretion of the public prosecutor, and the court will not attempt to control him in the exercise of that discretion, but they apprise the public prosecutor that if a motion had been made by the counsel of the prisoner, for her discharge on the ground of want of testimony, the motion would have been denied. If the testimony which remained was of a slight nature, it might have been cause for resting the prosecution upon the evidence already adduced; but not for discharging the prisoner.

Robert Livingston, DA

Under this intimation from the court without having changed my opinion. I wish to be considered as resting the prosecution, and call upon the prisoner for her defence.

Mr. Foot.

There is some difference of opinion, perfectly kind and friendly however, between the District Attorney and myself. In consequence of the suggestions of the court he now appears to be willing to put the prisoner on her defence. Satisfied myself that she cannot and ought not to be convicted upon the testimony given, and which is still in the power of the public prosecutor to produce, I have advised that the prosecution be abandoned, considering the intimation of the court only as an expression of opinion that in a criminal case the jury should be permitted to pronounce upon the evidence, instead of stopping the trial by the court, as is done in civil cases when there is a defeat of testimony.

Mr. Van Vechten observed, that after the candid admissions made by the counsel for the prosecution, the counsel for the prisoner would not call any witnesses, though they had it in their power to show strong explanatory testimony, and would submit the case to the charge of the court.

Judge Duer, after adverting to what had just transpired, and without recapitulating the testimony, submitted the case to the consideration of the jury. He cautioned them to look only at the evidence which affected the guilt or innocence of the prisoner in relation to the charge for which she was upon trial.

As to the evidence which had been adduced, showing her guilt in other respects, he advised them to say to her, as the court were disposed to say, and as the Saviour of the world said upon another occasion, "Go, and sin no more."

The jury, without leaving their seats, found a verdict of NOT GUILTY.

Again an expression of approbation was manifested; but it was immediately restrained. The unfortunate prisoner received the verdict of the jury, leaning forward, her face resting upon the table.

She neither looked up, nor seemed to be moved by the abrupt transition in the aspect of the trial which followed the decision of the court; nor did she appear to be particularly affected upon the rendering of the verdict, though she held her handkerchief to her face and wept; and when, under the proper and strong admonition of the court, that no unfeeling and curious eye should attempt to penetrate the thick veil which concealed her countenance from general observation, she left the chamber, she seemed to move mechanically and by the attending officers, rather than by any volition of her own.

Friday, August 3, 1827

The Troy Sentinel

THE TRIAL OF MRS. WHIPPLE

On Monday last, about 1 o'clock this wretched woman was brought again to the bar and the whole of the remainder of the day was continued in completing the panel of jurors. On Tuesday, at 9 o'clock the trial was opened, the court stating to the jury that the prisoner, Elsie D. Whipple, stood indicted for a felony, as accessary, before the fact, to the murder of her husband John Whipple; and the District Attorney reading the indictment, and stating the facts which he should give in evidence. When the first witness on the part of the prosecution, was called, Mr. Van Vechten, of counsel for the prisoner, objected that before any testimony could be offered to prove that the prisoner was accessary, the conviction of the principal must be legally proved, which, he contended, could not be done in any other way, than by exhibiting the record of the conviction of the principal. The objection was argued by counsel, and sustained by the court. After some little delay, a record of the trial of Strang with the verdict of guilty entered thereon, was then produced. Mr. E. Williams, also of counsel for the prisoner, then objected that the record was not sufficient without setting forth the sentence, and he cited authorities. This objection, however, after solemn argument was overruled, and the examination of witnesses was resumed.

While the first witness Abram L. Van Rensselaer was under examination, the District Attorney offered him, for his perusal to refresh his memory, an affidavit which he had made before a police justice, some time after the facts took place. Upon the objection of counsel that this was not a memorandum made at the time, but written down by a magistrate upon an ex parte examination, and, therefore, not such a paper as a witness might refer to, the court decided that the paper could not be used; and the examination proceeded. Three other witnesses were examined, and the court adjourned till the next morning.

The progress of the trial was interrupted, on Wednesday afternoon, by the appearance, in the N.Y. American and N.Y. Com. Advertiser, of the report of the trial of Strang at full length, contrary to an order of the court heretofore noticed, that no report of either trial should be published until both should be finished.

Upon the evidence being presented to the court of the violation of this order, the Judge made an order that Charles King and William L. Stone appear before the court and shew cause why an attachment should not be issued against them for contempt of court.
