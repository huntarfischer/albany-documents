## Tuesday May 8, 1827

The Albany Argus & City Gazette

PROCLAMATION

Whereas John Whipple, one of our citizens, was fired at and killed last night, between the hours of 9 and 10 o'clock, by some person unknown: I do therefore, by the advice and direction of the common council of the city of Albany, offer a reward of $500 for the apprehension of the said murderer, to be paid upon conviction, and upon the certificate of the district attorney, or the officer acting in behalf of this state upon the trial, that the person or persons claiming such reward is or are entitled to the same under this proclamation. In witness whereof I have hereunto set my hand, and caused the city seal to be affixed, this 8th day of May, 1827.

JAMES STEVENSON,

Mayor of the City of Albany.

## Wednesday, May 9, 1827

The Albany Argus & City Gazette

HORRIBLE ASSASINATION

One of the most horrible instances of deliberate assassination that we have ever been called upon to record, was committed in this vicinity on Monday evening. The particulars of this transaction, as they have been communicated to us, are as follows:

Mr. John Whipple, of this city, whilst sitting in a back room in the second story of his dwelling house on Cherry Hill, a mile below the city, between 9 and 10 o'clock at night, was fired at through the sash, with a pistol. The ball passed through and shattered a pane of glass, entered the body of Mr. Whipple through the blade bone of his left shoulder, cut one of the principal arteries of the heart, and lodged in the right lobe of the lungs; he barely exclaimed, "My God!" rose and walked a few steps to the head of the stairs, and there fell and immediately expired.

The murderer approached his victim by clambering on the roof of a back stoop, and firing from it diagonally at the distance of 10 feet. At the time of the shot, a young gentleman was sitting in the room with the deceased; but in the confusion of the moment no pursuit could be made; nor was any measure taken until information was conveyed to the police of the city. The steps of the villain are plainly visible on the roof of the porch, as is also the flash of the powder on the frame of the sash.

Mr. Whipple was in the prime of life, industrious, enterprising, and fair in all his transactions. He was respected as a valuable and intelligent citizen. He has been cut off in the midst of his usefulness, leaving a bereaved wife and an interesting young son to lament his untimely fate. Mr. Whipple. returned from New York, on Sunday night, where he had completed an advantageous contract connected with the Hudson and Delaware canal, in the construction of which he had participated largely; and was at the moment of his death arranging his papers for a departure from the city yesterday morning.

As it may well be conceived, an affair so atrocious and unusual amongst us, has excited great feeling and indignation; and no effort will be spared to detect the assassin.— The corporation of the city, it will be perceived, has offered a reward for that purpose. No cause, except upon conjecture, has been assigned for the commission of this deed. The circumstances under which it was perpetrated,—in the most populous environs of the city, in a dwelling where were several individuals and particularly in the face of a person sitting by the side of the deceased; after clambering also with much difficulty to an elevation equal with the second story, and all this early in a bright moon light evening,—mark it as one of the boldest, as it is one of the blackest, in the annals of crime.

Two witnesses were under examination yesterday afternoon before J. O. Cole, Esq., at the police office, the result of which is not known, only so far as they disclose nothing definite.

## Saturday, May 12, 1827

The Albany Argus & City Gazette

THE LATE MURDER

The examinations which have been going on during the last forty-eight hours relative to the late murder in this city, have not as yet resulted in the disclosure of any thing positive; though it is probable the person who has been chiefly the subject of them will be committed for trial. The affair is involved in much mystery, which the examinations have not by any means tended to diminish. Meanwhile nearly every citizen partakes more or less of the public feeling and excitement.

## Monday, May 14, 1827

The Albany Argus & City Gazette

A person by the assumed name of Joseph Orton, arrested on suspicion of being the murderer of Mr. Whipple, and whose examination was alluded to in this paper on Saturday, has been fully committed for trial, under strong presumption of guilt. The circumstances of this case, if the half is true that is reported, are revolting enough. We forbear, however, to go into the particulars, until they shall be officially divulged. The trial of the prisoner will take place at the circuit court for this county on the last Tuesday in August next, unless a special session shall be ordered in the meantime.

## Monday, May 14, 1827

THE POUGHKEEPSIE JOURNAL

THE MURDER AT ALBANY

We have verbal intelligence from Albany down to yesterday morning—The examinations in regard to the assassination of Mr. Whipple were proceeding. Orton, the inmate of the house, who seems to have been wearing the disguise, and acting in the assumed character of a labourer, has been examined and committed. The wife of the deceased, a very pretty, but said to be rather a weak woman, was examined on Saturday, and the circumstantial evidence thus far elicited, is of an appalling nature. But we forbear to state the facts which are said to have transpired, lest, in a case so fearful, our informants may have led us into error. The mails of to-morrow morning will doubtless give us some further light upon the subject.

## Tuesday, May 15, 1827

The Evening Post

It is ascertained that the true name of the person confined in gaol at Albany, on suspicion of murdering Mr. Whipple, is Jesse Strang.

## Wednesday, May 16, 1827

The Albany Argus & City Gazette

The corpse of the murdered Mr. Whipple was conveyed yesterday afternoon from Cherry Hill to the cemetery of the Episcopal church, in the west suburbs of this city. Notwithstanding the rain, a large concourse of people attended. The person arrested on Wednesday, on suspicion of being the murderer, has not yet been fully examined. He remains in confinement.

## Wednesday, May 16, 1827

The Albany Daily Advertiser

MYSTERY UNRAVELED

Some time in the latter part of 1824, or beginning of 1825, (we believe) a horse was found dead in the woods in the vicinity of Cayuga Lake, and a pair or saddle bags near him, cut open and rifled. It was then supposed that the owner of the horse, whose name was Jesse Strang, had been robbed and murdered, and his body thrown in the lake, and much time and pains were taken by hundreds of people in dragging the lake in search of the body. From that time until last Friday, there had been no intelligence of the lost man. On that day, a gentleman from that county, went to the jail in this city, and the moment he saw Strang, the supposed murderer of Mr. Whipple, he knew him to be the person before mentioned, and on charging him with the fact, Strang acknowledged it, and laughing heartily at the idea of dragging the lake in search of him. The reason for his practicing this deception, is said to be, that he had forged several drafts, (one of which was cashed in this city) and he wished to be thought dead, to avoid being discovered. Since that time he passed by the name of Joseph Orton.

## Thursday, May 17, 1827

The Albany Argus & City Gazette

THE LATE MURDER

We perceive that several of the New-York papers give many of the alleged circumstances connected with the late murder in this city. These are the versions of the reports on this subject which are now in general circulation; and which, though correct in some respects, are nevertheless liable to exaggeration and inaccuracy. It may be doubted, under such circumstances, whether any good purpose can be served by stating, even if it should prove to be true, all that is now afloat. It is perhaps enough to say, that Orton, or Strang, has been fully committed for trial, under very strong presumption of guilt, and that the circumstantial proof against him has been strengthened rather than diminished since his committal; but that however criminal Mrs. Whipple's conduct may have been in other respects, no proof exists, after two examinations, of a participation on her part in the murder.

## Saturday, May 19, 1827

THE ANTIDOTE

ALARMING

Is there not an alarming state of morals? And what has become of the meekness and piety, the practical morality and sound faith of our progenitors, that so many horrid deeds are perpetrated among us? The murders of Sharp, of Moore, and of Whipple, what do they announce, but the absence of religion, the reign of resistless, vicious and malignant passions. Many other murders, and acts of violence, have stained our land within a year past; and would any of these have taken place had Christianity animated the breasts of the perpetrators. The answer is obvious. These things show, that without religion, human laws lose their force, and society is deprived of its strongest bulwark. How, then, can reflecting men, by their speculative opinions what they may, set about seriously to untrain the minds of the multitude from the doctrines of Christianity? They are “scattering firebrands, arrows and death,” in every direction; and ten to one but they may yet become the victims of that false philosophy, that delusion, which they are taking so much pain to extend the empire. How could Marat expect to escape the dagger of Charlotte, when he had himself taught her there was no God, that religion was a farce, an imposition, and death an eternal sleep. He injured her deeply, and then taught her that there was no future state; and she of course feared no future punishment for murdering him. Thus it is that infidelity digs its own pit, drinks its own poison to the dregs, falls into its own snare, and is destroyed by its own means. When will infidelity see its error? When will it learn, that Christianity is our bond of safety here—the sheet-anchor of our hopes hereafter.

## Tuesday May 22, 1827

The Troy Register

NEW CHANNEL OF TRADE

It will be seen that, with a view to supply their West India colonies with American flour, and to give full employment to British tonnage, to the total exclusion of American shipping, which is shut out from the West India trade, the British government has opened the ports of Canada to the free admission of flour from the United States. This, we consider the heaviest blow that was ever aimed at the commercial interest of the State of New York, and its effects will be severely felt by this city and Albany, as it at once diverts from us the immense business which the opening of the Erie Canal promised to this city during the present season. And this is not all; the revenue from the tolls on the Erie Canal will also be very much diminished in consequence of the new direction which the vast quantities of flour manufactured at Rochester and other places to the west will take through the St. Lawrence, instead of coming to the Hudson river. Whether a remedy to this mighty evil is within the powers vested in the President of the United States, we know not; but, if he has the power, we hope and trust he will exercise it immediately, by prohibiting the exportation of flour thus indirectly to the British colonies in the West Indies in a way so detrimental, nay ruinous, to the interests of ship owners in the United States. We are heartily tired of procrastinated negotiations and halfway measures. Some decisive measures should be adopted to meet and counteract the insidious policy of a power ever on the watch to check and retard the progress of our commercial enterprise.

## Wednesday May 23, 1827

The New York Commercial Advertiser

THE ALBANY MURDER

From the Rochester Telegraph—The evident concealment by the Albany papers, of suspicions almost too horrible to be entertained, induces us to apprehend that Mr. Whipple was assassinated under circumstances of atrocity, which, although not unparalleled, causes humanity to weep over the frailty of its nature. It was, an ill-sorted marriage, but we never anticipated such appalling fruits as these from it.

Mrs. Whipple was the orphan grand-daughter and ward of an illiberal miserly old churl, by whom she was denied the advantages of society and education to which her fortune entitled her. Her property, it is fair to presume, attracted suitors, from all of whom, however, it was understood, her guardian discouraged her. She grew impatient of restraint, and an union was hastily arranged between her and Mr. Whipple, by a female friend, and the parties eloped to Vermont and were married.

If our conjectures should prove to be correct, and we fear they are but too well founded, the guardian of Mrs. Whipple will have a fearful responsibility resting upon him. Had she been properly educated, all these horrors would have been averted, for she was naturally mild and amiable. Mr. Whipple was about 25 and Mrs. Whipple about 15 when they were married in 1817.

## Thursday, May 31, 1827

The Chenango Republican

SHOCKING AFFAIR

At about 12 o'clock yesterday a horrible affair occurred in a house in Water street next to the corner of Old slip. A man named Alexander M'Lean, a Labourer in several marble stores in the lower part of the city, stabbed his wife, which caused her death in less than an hour afterward. They had been separated nearly two months in consequence of ill treatment on his part.

The husband is represented as having been addicted to intemperance and to have used his wife (who is stated to have been an industrious woman, possessing an exemplary and fair character) in an abusive and cruel manner. A child, aged four years, was their sole offspring, and was present at the commission of the horrid deed. M'Lean presented himself before his wife at about 12 yesterday, having visited her twice previously in the course of the forenoon, and inflicted, after a short altercation, the wound which caused her death. A young girl, was in the room at the time, hearing a shriek, turned around and saw M'Lean in the act of withdrawing a knife from the body of his wife. Being alarmed she ran down stairs to call her mother, and was followed by the unfortunate woman, who exclaimed that "M'Lean had killed her!" Two men who happened to be in the apartment below, caught her in their arms, carried her to the room above and placed her on a bed upon which she shortly after expired. M'Lean having mixed a dose of poison, which he had ineffectually desired his wife to take, swallowed part of it immediately after the commission of the act. He was after a short delay apprehended and carried to Bridewell, where medicines were administered and at a late hour last evening he was believed to be relieved from its effects and likely to survive. The coroner's jury returned a verdict of "Death from a wound inflicted by Alexander M'Lean, with a butcher's knife."

As this melancholy affair will of course come before a jury, we deem it improper to enter into a detail of the circumstances which are supposed to have produced the fatal result. Suffice it to say that jealousy (groundless as is said) is said to be the cause of his having committed this horrid act.

We understand, says the Evening Post, that the wretch attempted the life of his child by introducing arsenic into an orange which he gave her, part of which she ate, and in the course of last evening sickened. Medical attendance was sent for, and remedies administered, which had the desired effect of removing the poison.

We have just learned that the monster M'Lean died in his prison this forenoon.
